A hare once boasted that no creature in the forest could match his speed. A tortoise, tired of the boasting, offered a race. All the animals gathered to watch. The hare shot ahead and, certain of victory, lay down beneath a tree for a nap. The tortoise walked on, slow and steady, never stopping. When the hare woke, the tortoise was already crossing the finish line. The animals cheered, and the hare learned that steady effort beats careless speed.

An ant spent the summer gathering grain and storing it away for the cold months. A grasshopper spent the same summer singing in the sun, mocking the ant for working so hard. When winter came, the grasshopper had nothing to eat and knocked on the ant's door, asking for food. The ant shared a little, but reminded the grasshopper that seasons of plenty do not last forever, and that those who prepare are rarely caught without.

A shepherd boy grew bored watching his flock and decided to shout that a wolf was attacking. The villagers ran to help, only to find the sheep safe and the boy laughing. He played the same trick twice more, and each time the villagers came running for nothing. One day a wolf truly did appear among the sheep. The boy shouted as loud as he could, but no one believed him anymore, and the flock was lost. Trust, once spent carelessly, is hard to recover.

A crow found a piece of cheese and perched on a branch to enjoy it. A fox, hungry and clever, admired the crow loudly, praising its feathers and its voice, and asked to hear it sing. Flattered, the crow opened its beak to caw, and the cheese fell straight into the fox's waiting mouth. The fox trotted off satisfied, leaving the crow to understand that flattery often hides an appetite.

A lion, resting in the shade, was disturbed by a small mouse running across his paw. The lion caught the mouse and prepared to eat it, but the mouse begged for mercy, promising that one day it might return the favor. The lion laughed at the idea but let the mouse go. Some time later, the lion was caught in a hunter's net. The mouse heard his roars, found him, and gnawed through the ropes until he was free. Even the smallest friend can prove the most useful.

Two travelers walking through the woods spotted a bear in the distance. One climbed a tree at once and hid among the branches, leaving his companion behind. The other, unable to climb, dropped to the ground and held his breath, having heard that bears do not touch what appears already dead. The bear sniffed at him, walked in a slow circle, and moved on. When it was safe, the first traveler climbed down and asked what the bear had whispered. The other replied that the bear had advised him to choose better companions, ones who do not run at the first sign of danger.

A farmer's sons quarreled constantly, each blaming the others for every small trouble on the farm. Their father, tired of the arguing, handed them a bundle of sticks tied tightly together and asked each son to break it. None could. He then untied the bundle and gave them each a single stick, which broke easily in their hands. He told them that alone, each of them could be broken just as easily, but bound together as a family, they could withstand almost anything.

A fox, walking through an orchard, spotted a bunch of grapes hanging from a high vine. He jumped again and again, straining to reach them, but the grapes stayed just out of range. Exhausted, he finally gave up and walked away, muttering that the grapes were probably sour anyway and not worth the trouble. It is easy to scorn what we cannot have.

A dog carrying a piece of meat crossed a bridge over a calm stream and glanced down. In the water he saw another dog holding what looked like a larger piece of meat. Greedy for more, he opened his mouth to snap at the reflection, and his own meat fell into the water and was lost. He learned, too late, that reaching for more can cost what one already holds.

A wolf, dressed in an old sheepskin he had found, slipped in among a flock of sheep and moved with them undetected for days, waiting patiently. The shepherd, seeing what he believed was simply another sheep, thought nothing of it. That night, the wolf chose his moment and helped himself to the flock, disappearing before dawn. Appearances, however convincing, do not always match what lies beneath them.

A milkmaid walked to market balancing a pail of milk on her head, imagining all the things she would buy once it was sold: eggs, then chickens from the eggs, then a fine dress from the chickens, and admiring glances from everyone in the village. Caught up in the daydream, she tossed her head proudly, and the pail tipped and spilled every drop onto the road. Counting rewards before they arrive is a habit that rarely ends well.

Two frogs lived together in a small pond that dried up one summer. Searching for a new home, they came across a deep well full of water. One frog wanted to jump straight in, pleased with how easy it looked. The other stopped him, asking what they would do if that well ever dried up too, since there would be no way to climb back out. It is worth thinking of the way out before jumping into anything new.

A fisherman spent a whole day at the lake and caught nothing but a single small fish. The fish begged to be thrown back, promising to grow much larger if given time, and suggested he would be a far better catch later. The fisherman shook his head and dropped the small fish into his basket anyway, saying that a small certainty now was worth more than a large promise later.

A stag admired his tall, branching antlers reflected in a pool of water, but frowned at his own thin legs, which he found unimpressive by comparison. When hunters and hounds suddenly appeared, his legs carried him swiftly through open ground, far ahead of any danger. It was only when he ran into a thicket that his prized antlers caught in the branches, holding him fast until the hounds arrived. What we admire most is not always what saves us, and what we overlook is not always without value.
